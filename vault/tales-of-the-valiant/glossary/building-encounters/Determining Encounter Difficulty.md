## Determining Encounter Difficulty
```rpg rule.side
type: rules
title: Understanding action economy
---
The number of monsters in a combat encounter has the biggest effect on the encounter’s difficulty. The comparison of the number of total actions the PCs can take on a turn compared to the number of total actions the monsters can take on a turn is “action economy.” Single monsters, even at higher CRs, are at a significant disadvantage against a group of PCs, because they have so few actions compared to all those available to a group of PCs. Likewise, when there are twice as many monsters as PCs, those PCs are at a disadvantage— even against weaker monsters. Groups with fewer creatures also represent fewer overall targets than a group with more creatures, making it easier to focus on these fewer enemies and end them quickly. Keep action economy in mind when building combat encounters. When the PCs have significantly more actions than their foes, they will be at a big advantage. If they have _fewer_ actions than the monsters, the battle will be harder.
```
No table, chart, or mathematical equation can exactly describe how difficult an encounter will be during actual gameplay. There are too many variables in any combat encounter to know precisely how a challenge will play out, but understanding the variables will help you make a more accurate guess. These variables include: 
- **Number of PCs.** More PCs have greater survivability and capability than fewer PCs, no matter the average PC level. 
- **Synergy of PCs.** PC classes (or subclasses) can create powerful combinations when matched with each other. A party full of high-synergy pairings may need more of a challenge than a standard encounter challenge for their level.  
- **Environment.** Some environments favor PCs or monsters, depending on the situation. In particular, some creatures have lair actions—powerful abilities a creature can use only while on its own home turf. 
- **Experience Level of Players.** Experienced players who have played through many combat encounters can be extra effective when working as a team, regardless of the level of the PCs they play. 
- **Magic Items, Spells, Abilities, and NPC Allies.** Some magic items, spells, and other PC abilities can dramatically change the difficulty of a battle. Similarly, any allied NPCs helping the party will lower the overall difficulty of combat encounters. 
- **Monster Combinations.** Some monsters work particularly well with others, making them more dangerous than their individual CRs might suggest. 
- **Well-Rested PCs.** PCs have more resources at their disposal when well rested compared to when they’ve already weathered a few battles. A party at full health, with all spells and healing resources at their disposal, can handle far more challenging encounters than that same party operating at partial power. 

Keep in mind that any general encounter building guidelines are superseded by your experiences with your players. Understanding the patterns and capabilities of your group is critical to creating perfectly tailored challenges! 

### Understanding Encounter Difficulty
```rpg rule.side
type: rules
title: Calculating average PC Level
---
Some of these calculations require that you know the average PC level for the group. If the PCs in your game are all the same level, the average party level is equal to that level. If, however, you have a group of PCs with different levels, you can determine the average party level by adding up all PCs levels and dividing this number by the number of PCs.
```
The following guidelines give you a gauge of encounter difficulty by setting clear benchmarks where combat encounters are likely to be challenging or dangerous. A benchmark functions like a warning gauge, indicting the point where a combat encounter becomes more difficult or deadly. The lower an encounter is under a given benchmark, the easier it is. The higher an encounter is over the benchmark, the more difficult it is. 

Don’t build all your combat encounters to be exactly at a given benchmark. The benchmark is designed to help determine when encounters might be challenging; it isn’t a model to follow for every battle. While preparing encounters, mix easier encounters of a few low CR monsters along with more challenging battles against bigger or more numerous monsters. Mixing up encounter difficulty keeps your players better engaged, allowing them to enjoy the fun of devastating weaker foes and the thrill of struggling against challenging opponents.
#### Calculating Challenging Encounter Benchmark
To find your group’s challenging encounter benchmark, add up all the levels of the PCs in your group. Use that number to calculate your party’s challenging encounter benchmark with one of the following equations:
- If the PCs are 1st–4th level, your benchmark is equal to 1/4 of total PC levels (rounded down) 
- If the PCs are 5th–10th level, your benchmark is equal to 1/2 of total PC levels (rounded down) 
- If the PCs are 11th–20th level, your benchmark is equal to 3/4 of total PC levels (rounded down) 

For example, if you have five 7th level PCs, their total PC levels equal 35. Because they’re 7th level, their challenging encounter benchmark equals 1/2 of their total levels, which makes the party’s challenging encounter benchmark 17 (1/2 of 35 rounded down). If you have six characters of 3rd level, their total PC levels is 18, and the challenging benchmark is 4 (1/4 of 18 rounded down). If you have five characters of 13th level, their total PC levels is 65, and the challenging benchmark is 48 (3/4 of 65 rounded down).  
#### Applying the Benchmark
To gauge a combat’s difficulty, add up all the CRs of each individual monster you plan to have in the encounter, then compare that number to your party’s challenging encounter benchmark. If the monster total CRs add up to a number larger than the benchmark, the battle is potentially difficult or dangerous. The higher the total CR number is above your benchmark, the more dangerous the battle is likely to be. Likewise, the lower the total CR number is below the benchmark, the less challenging the battle might be. 

If the PCs in your group have powerful NPC sidekicks or seem to have too easy a time, pretend the group has an additional PC of the average party level. This increases the overall challenging benchmark as though the group is bigger than it is. 
#### Determining Maximum CR for a Single Monster
The challenging encounter benchmark defines the benchmark for a _group_ of monsters. The benchmark doesn’t determine the maximum CR of any single monster in the encounter. The highest CR for a challenging monster in a combat encounter is equal to the average level of the PCs for PCs of 2nd to 4th level, or 1.5 × the average level of the PCs if they’re 5th level or above. 

For example, the highest CR for any one of the monsters facing the group of five 7th level characters mentioned above is 10 (1.5 x 7 rounded down). The highest CR for a monster facing five 13th level PCs is 19. The highest CR for a challenging monster for the group of six 3rd level PCs is 3—since they’re below 5th level. 

You can go above the single monster CR number if the situation makes sense or if you know the PCs can defeat higher CR foes than this. Again, your own experience outweighs these formulas. 
### Challenging Benchmark Table
The challenging encounter benchmark guidance earlier in this section is intended to be simple enough that you can do it in your head and write down the benchmark in your notes. Alternatively, the **Challenging Benchmarks by Average Level** table offers the combined monster CR total for a challenging encounter per average PC level. It also shows the minimum and maximum CRs for challenging monsters for each average level. 

To use this table, find the row with the average level of your PCs. Then find the column with the number of PCs in the group. The number in that cell is the number of combined monster CRs for a challenging encounter. The Min CR column describes the likely minimum CR for a single creature that can pose a challenge to the PCs at this level. Creatures below this CR pose little to no challenge. The Max CR describes the likely maximum CR for a single creature the PCs can handle. Creatures above this CR could be extremely dangerous or deadly to the PCs at this level.  

If you find encounters on this chart to be too difficult for your party, pretend there is one less PC of the average PC level and use that column. Likewise, if encounters prove too easy with this chart, pretend there is one additional PC of the average PC level and use that column.
