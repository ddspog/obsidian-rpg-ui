This chapter covers a suite of tools to customize existing mechanics or to handcraft entirely new options for your game. Whether you want to design new spells, magic items, PC options, or monsters, this toolbox provides you with direct guidance and actionable steps to make exactly what you need. 

The guidance in this chapter assumes you have access to the **Tales of the Valiant** _Player’s Guide_ or _Monster Vault_ or _Black Flag Roleplaying Reference Document_, which contain the full definitions and descriptions of many of the concepts and terms found in this chapter. For example, the section related to creating magic items assumes you know the definition of the term attunement, and the section related to creating monsters assumes you know the definition of challenge rating and how to use it to determine a creature’s proficiency bonus. 
# What is Homebrewing? 

In the world of games, “homebrew” broadly refers to any material produced by users of a game, typically as independent or private projects and as additional material to enhance their own games at home. If you have ever played a video game with a mod or ever played a fan “hack” of a game, you have used homebrew content. In tabletop RPGs, much of the additional content GMs create for their games—from unique worlds to “house” rules—is considered homebrew content. 

This chapter includes tools and guidance for GMs looking to create their own unique content for their games. For GMs interested in creating content for public consumption, we recommend checking out _Black Flag Roleplaying Reference Document_ and _Kobold Guide to Game Design 2nd Edition_. Such content creation is otherwise outside the scope of this chapter.
# Rules Design Best Practices 

When designing new content for your game, there are a handful of general guidelines to keep in mind. 

**_Confirm Design is Necessary._** Designing new things can be fun, but it can also be time-consuming, pulling a GM’s attention away from preparing the session, crafting a story, or building a game world. Ask yourself if creating new game mechanics or effects are necessary. Can you accomplish the same goal from modifying existing content instead, saving yourself the extra work? If not, then this is the chapter for you. 

**_Define a Purpose._** Everything you design should have a purpose. Ask yourself how the new game mechanics or effects fit into your game. It can be fun to put together theoretical aspects of the game to see how they function together. However, when you finally settle on creating something, be sure to have a clear purpose for that something, whether that purpose is to enhance an aspect of your story, tell its own story about an aspect of your game world, or delight your PCs with a never-before-seen treasure or enemy. 

**_Have a Theme._** The easiest way to get across an idea about new game material, especially when presenting it to others, is to have a clear theme. A winged hippopotamus isn’t nearly as evocative or clear in theme as the winged hippopotamus guardian of the river goddess’s temple. A theme will also help you during the creation process, allowing you to ask yourself questions like, “Is fire breath an in-theme feature, or do I just like the idea of a fire-breathing hippo?” The good news is that the theme can change throughout the process. Sure, the winged hippopotamus guardian can have a fire breath, especially if the river goddess is also a sun goddess. The important thing is to make sure you have a theme that each aspect of the game material you are creating points back toward or showcases in some way. 
# Creating Character Options 

![[creating-character-options.webp|right|384]]Creating unique character options is often the best way to integrate PCs into a specific game world or setting. A player whose PC is built using elements unique to that world is more likely to feel like they have a place in it. Creating these options can also be a great way for a worldbuilding GM to better fill out their world since it encourages thinking about the kinds of peoples there and how PCs might interact with them. 

This section provides guidance on creating new lineages, heritages, backgrounds, talents, and subclasses. When creating such aspects, always keep in mind their purpose in your game, their role in the overarching game world, and what aspect of the game world or story they are expanding. Does the aquatic lineage showcase the vast underwater realms? Is the heritage you’re creating meant to tell the PCs a story about a particular culture? 
## Creating Lineages
Lineage is about a PC’s inherited biological traits. No matter where the character is born, they are born like this. Things that typically go into a lineage are innate powers (magical or otherwise) and physical features, such as darkvision or wings that allow flight. 

When creating a lineage, define the lineage’s age—which includes age at adulthood and average life expectancy—its size, and its speed. Then create traits that define any special senses or similar physical or biological characteristics it has. If the lineage has branching biology, such as in the beastkin lineage, give the lineage the Natural Adaptation trait with descriptions of each branch’s unique aspect.
## Creating Heritages
Heritage is a PC’s learned traits. It is about the cultural elements that influenced a character during their formative years. It encompasses what the character’s family, community, or other formative authority figure taught them. Things that typically go into a heritage are behavioral powers, skill proficiencies, tool proficiencies, weapon proficiencies, languages, and learned magical abilities, such as the ability to cast a specific spell. 

When creating a heritage, choose any languages those of that heritage speak. Then create traits that define the kinds of weapons or armor training, skill and tool use, or spellcasting those raised within that heritage would have been taught.
## Creating Backgrounds
Backgrounds are how a PC chose to spend their time before they became an adventurer. Some mechanical expressions can overlap with heritage, but think of a background as what the character might learn professionally as opposed to what they might learn from their personal life. 

Backgrounds have a structure with the following headings. When creating a background, fill in each of these sections with the listed information. 

**_Background Proficiencies._** A background typically includes two skill proficiencies and one or two tool proficiencies, languages, or vehicle proficiencies. 

**_Background Equipment._** A background typically includes a short list of four to six nonmagical items a person of the background would need or might have collected to be successful. This list always includes a set of clothes. 

**_Background Talents._** A background always includes a list of three talents related to the background, and the PC must choose one of them. This list typically includes talents from different categories to offer variety in the talent options. For more about talents and their categories, see **Creating Talents** later in this section. 

**_Adventuring Motivation._** A background includes an adventuring motivation that helps a PC explain why someone of their knowledge and talents would seek a life of adventure, danger, and uncertainty. 
## Creating Talents
Talents are divided into three categories: magic, martial, and technical. When creating a talent, choose one of these categories, which are each defined here, and build features that fit within that category’s theme and parameters. 

**_Prerequisites._** Though not all talents have prerequisites, many of them have prerequisites that help further define the types of characters that should have access to them. Each of the categories detailed here includes what kinds of prerequisites sometimes appear on those types of talents. 

**_Be Specific._** Though many talents can be broadly applicable, this is the place for that niche, hyper-specialized ability that allows a PC to focus on a type of weapon, set of tools, magic school, or skill and gain new, unique features based on that focus. Talents can be almost as character‑defining as subclasses since they often represent a special interest or area of expertise. 
### Magic
Most magic talents affect a PC’s spellcasting abilities, but they also include features that strengthen the mind and defend against harmful magic effects. 

**_Prerequisites._** When prerequisites appear on magic talents, they typically require the PC to have access to spells of specific circles, access to spellcasting in general, or access to specific types of spells, such as those that deal damage or restore hit points. 
### Martial
Martial talents affect a PC’s combat abilities, including enhancements to fitness and the ability to wield weaponry. 

**_Prerequisites._** When prerequisites appear on martial talents, they typically require the PC to have a minimum STR or DEX score, have proficiency in a type of armor or weapon, or have a minimum character level. 
### Technical
Technical talents affect a PC’s noncombat abilities, granting utility-based improvements to social encounters, exploration, and object interactions. 

**_Prerequisites._** When prerequisites appear on technical talents, they typically require the PC to have a minimum ability score or proficiency in a skill or tool.
