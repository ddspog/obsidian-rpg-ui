While many adventures involve riding through tree-dotted emerald-green expanses or trudging over frost-rimed mountains, others take PCs to even stranger places. They might prowl the trade lanes in a galleon with sails of stitched dragonhide, preying on enemy ships in the name of the crown. They might ride on the backs of giant owls to infiltrate a giant’s keep built atop the churning clouds of an ever-raging storm. Or they might drift through the deepest reaches of the planes in a vessel crafted of a dead god’s viscera. Whether in a dungeon, a parched wasteland, or a village sucked into a realm of pure shadow, adventures take place in a wide range of locales and environments, some of which react differently than the ones the PCs are accustomed to.

This chapter is a toolbox for adventure. Filled with unusual environments, puzzles, and even rules for building dungeons, you have everything you need to present your players with exploration that is just as vivid and tense as combat encounters.

# Additional Rules  
The following rules can further enhance exploration in the game. These can be used in conjunction with the basic exploration rules presented in the _Player’s Guide_.
## Weather
Tracking and incorporating weather in the game can create a more immersive experience for the players, especially in campaigns that heavily feature travel and exploration. Extreme weather can dramatically increase the difficulty of wilderness encounters, especially if weather conditions are severe enough to trigger hazards, such as those presented in the _Player’s Guide_ or in [[07. Adventuring Options|Chapter 7]]. 

When your PCs first enter a new region, roll on the **Temperature**, **Wind**, and **Precipitation** tables to determine the immediate weather conditions for the area or choose something appropriate. Then roll 1d20 and consult the **Weather Changes** table to determine how the weather will change over time. Typically, rolling once per day is enough. However, at your discretion, more unstable areas or unique conditions might demand more frequent rolls.

## Traveling Expanded
This sections present additional rules for long‑distance travel, expanding on the rules in the Player’s Guide.
### Journey Stages
![[travel-planner.webp|right|384]] It can be helpful to break up a journey into stages, with each stage representing anything from a few hours of journey to ten days or so of travel. A journey might have only a single stage if the trip is a matter of following a clear path to a well-known destination. A journey consisting of three stages makes for a satisfying trek. For example, the characters might travel along a river to the forest's edge (stage 1), follow a trail into the heart of the woods (stage 2), and then search the woods for an ancient ruin (stage 3). A long journey might involve even more stages and occupy several game sessions.

You decide how to break up the journey, though your decision can be shaped by the characters' plan for navigating the journey. When the characters know the route they must take, the stages of the journey should correspond to the way you might give someone directions, as in the example above.

**Planning the Stages**. You can use the accompanying Travel Planner sheet to plan the stages of a journey. (Use multiple copies of the Travel Planner for a journey with more than three stages.)

For each stage, note where it starts and ends, the distance covered, and the predominant terrain. Choose or randomly determine the weather on that stage (see "Weather" later in this chapter). Plan one or more challenges for each stage, such as an encounter, an obstacle, a search for something hidden, or a chance of getting lost, as described under 'Journey Stage Challenges."
>[!rules] JOURNEYS WITHOUT DESTINATIONS
>Sometimes, characters travel without a clear path to follow or a clear destination in mind. ln such a case, use the grid of your map (squares or hexes) to define the stages of the journey, however many miles each square or hex might be. (This style of play is sometimes called "hex crawling.")
>ln this kind of wilderness exploration, you can take one oftwo approaches to travel challenges:
>
>**Sandbox Approach**. Your map of the area determines what characters find when they enter any particular hex on the map. You might have encounters or obstacles in place for every hex, or they could be spread farther apart.
>
>**Random Approach**. Use tables to randomly determine encounters or obstacles in each hex the characters enter.
>
>Whichever approach you use, running a journey otherwise works the same as described in the rest of the "Travel" section.

**Running the Stages**. For each stage of the journey, follow these steps in order:
- **Step 1: Set the Pace**. Have the players choose their group's travel pace for the stage: Slow, Normal, or Fast (see "Travel Pace"). Based on the length of the stage (in miles) and the group's pace, determine how long this stage takes to complete.
- **Step 2: Narrate the Travel**. Describe what happens as the characters complete this stage of their journey. Introduce and resolve any challenges (see [[05. Advanced Exploration#Travelling Challenges\|Travelling Challenges]]).
- **Step 3: Track Food and Water Consumption**. Each creature in the party expends the appropriate amount of food and water for the length of the stage. If the party lacks enough food or water, the characters risk dehydration and malnutrition.
- **Step 4: Track Progress**. Track the party's progress at the end of the stage. You might mark their position on a map of the region and note the elapsed time on the Travel Planner.

Depending on how you planned the stages, the end of a stage might mean the characters arrive at a landmark, a waystation, or an adventure location, whether or not it's their final destination.
### Marching Order
The PCs begin traveling by determining their marching order. Having a set order makes it easier to determine which PCs might be affected by lurking dangers, which have the best chance of spotting hidden threats, and which are closest to enemies in case a fight breaks out.

The number of ranks in a marching order will change based on the number of creatures in the party and how much space there is to travel side by side. For example, a wide stretch of open road offers ample space for creatures to walk side by side. In a confined dungeon corridor, creatures might be forced to walk single file. In general, there is typically at least a rank in front and a rank in back. Parties with more creatures typically also have one or more ranks in the middle.
### Travel Pace
A group of characters can travel overland at a Normal, Fast, or Slow pace, as described in the *Player's Guide*. During any journey stage, the predominant terrain determines the characters' maximum travel pace, as shown in the Maximum Pace column of the **Travel Terrain** table. Certain factors can affect a group's travel pace.

**Good Roads**. The presence of a good road increases the group's maximum pace by one step (from Slow to Normal or from Normal to Fast).

**Slower Travelers**. The group must move at a Slow pace if any group member's Speed is reduced to half or less of normal.

**Extended Travel**. Characters can push themselves to travel for more than 8 hours per day, at the risk of tiring. At the end of each additional hour of travel beyond 8 hours, each character must succeed on a Constitution saving throw or gain 1 [[Exhaustion]] level. The DC is 1O plus 1 for each hour past 8 hours.

**Special Movement**. lf a party can travel at a high Speed for an extended time, as with a spell such as [[Wind Walk]] or a magic item such as a [[Carpet of Flying]], translate the party's Speed into travel rates using these rules:

$Miles$ $per$ $hour = Speed / 10$
$Miles$ $per$ $day$ ($Normal$ $pace$) $=$ $Miles$ $per$ $hour$ $*$ $number$ $of$ $hours$ $traveled$ ($typically$ $8$)
$Fast$ $pace = Miles$ $per$ $day * 1 \frac{1}{3}$ ($round$ $down$)
$Slow$ $pace = Miles$ $per$ $day * \frac{2}{3} ($$round$ $down$)

If the characters are flying or their special movement allows them to ignore [[Difficult Terrain]], they can move at a Fast pace regardless of the terrain.

**Vehicles**. Characters traveling in a vehicle use the vehicle's speed in miles per hour (as shown in [[05. Equipment & Magic Items#Vehicles|Vehicles]]) to determine their rate of travel, and they don't choose a travel pace.

### Narration during Travel
![[narration-during-travel.webp]]Just as movies use travel montages to convey long and arduous journeys in a matter of seconds, you can use a few sentences of descriptive text to paint a picture of a journey in your players’ minds before moving on. Describe the journey as vividly as you like, but keep momentum by focusing on the most notable details that reinforce the desired mood.

Visual aids can help set the scene for the characters’ travels. Image searches on the internet can lead you to breathtaking landscapes (in fact, that’s a good phrase to search for). You can spice up your descriptions with truly fantastical elements. For example, a forest might be home to tiny dragonets instead of birds, or its trees might be festooned with giant webs or have eerie green, glowing sap. A single fantastic element within an otherwise realistic and memorable landscape is enough.

Use the landscape to set the mood and tone for your adventure. In one forest, close-set trees might shroud all light and seem to watch the adventurers as they pass. In another, sunlight streams through the leaves above, and flower-laden vines twine up every trunk. Signs of corruption—rotting wood, foul-smelling water, and rocks covered with slimy moss—can be a signal that the adventurers are drawing close to the site of evil power that is their destination or can provide clues to the nature of the threats to be found there.
### Stealth
![[traveling-stealth.webp|right|384]]While traveling at a slow pace, PCs can choose to move stealthily, potentially allowing the group to surprise creatures they come across (see [[Surprised]]). If a group is traveling at a fast or normal pace, they can’t travel stealthily and have no opportunity to hide from any creatures they might encounter.
### Perception
While traveling, use the PCs’ passive [[Perception]] scores to determine whether anyone in the group notices a hidden danger. Depending on the environment, you might decide that only PCs in a particular rank have a chance of noticing a threat. For example, you might determine only PCs in the front can notice an upcoming danger while the party is traveling through a cramped tunnel. Similarly, you might determine only PCs in the back have a chance of noticing a creature stalking them from behind.

While traveling at a fast pace, everyone in the party has a −5 penalty to their passive Perception scores.
>[!rules] PLAYER ADVICE
>Remember that PCs with the Far Traveler talent don’t suffer a −5 penalty to passive Perception when traveling at a fast pace.
## Travelling Challenges
Challenges that adventurers might face during a journey stage include the following, which are discussed in the sections that follow:
### Random Encounters
Random encounters are an excellent way to spice up long periods of travel. To incorporate them, choose a random encounter table from **Appendix A: Random Encounter Tables** that matches the area’s terrain type and the appropriate character level of the party. Alternatively, you can create your own random encounter table to tailor the encounters to a specific area or quest.

At the start of each day’s travel, roll 1d20, and on a roll of 16 or higher, there is a random encounter that day. This can happen at any point during the day that seems appropriate. You can lower or raise the target number on the roll to make encounters more or less likely, depending on the context of the setting and storyline. If the party travels through an especially dangerous area, you might want to roll for random encounters for each hour of travel.

Once you’ve determined a random encounter is imminent, then just roll on the appropriate table from **Appendix A** to flesh out that encounter.

The Encounter Distance column in the [[05. Advanced Exploration#Travel Terrain|Travel Terrain]] table gives the range at which creatures might become aware of each other while journeying through the wilderness. When staging an encounter, consider these possibilities:
- **Ambush.** Monsters set up an ambush along a route they expect travelers to follow.
- **Attack from Above.** Flying monsters swoop down to attack the characters.
- **Distant Sighting.** The characters and monsters spot each other from a distance in open terrain.
- **Found by Chance.** The characters happen upon monsters that are camping, eating, hunting, basking in the sun, walking along the same trail, or engaged in some other activity.
- **Pursuit.** The characters are tracking monsters, or the monsters are tracking them. The encounter begins when the two groups get close enough to interact.

However often you check for random encounters, be careful not to exhaust the players by stacking too many back-to-back. Travel is usually best enhanced by a handful of memorable encounters, rather than a slog of monster attacks.
### Getting Lost
PCs traveling off-road or without a map run the risk of becoming lost. Here are some circumstances that can cause a group to lose its way:
- Branching passages underground
- Horizon-obscuring terrain, such as dense forest
- Obscuring weather, such as heavy rain or fog
- Traveling at night
- Traveling at sea while unable to see the sky or any familiar land
 
While traveling in such areas, the players should elect one PC to serve as the navigator for the group. For each significant period of time spent traveling (typically once per day), the navigator must make a WIS ([[Navigator Tools]] or [[Survival]]) check to determine whether they have gotten off track. Consult the **Overland Navigation** table to determine the DC of the check based on the terrain and travel pace. A navigator that has proficiency in both navigator’s tools and Survival has advantage on their check. On a success, the party is on track to their destination.

If the check fails, the party has gotten lost and wastes a day traveling in the wrong direction. You decide what this looks like: they might follow the wrong branch of a river, orient themselves to the wrong mountain peak on the horizon, or get turned around in the forest. As a baseline, assume that getting lost extends the length of the current journey stage by 1d6 × 10 percent. It might also affect subsequent stages of the journey.

If the party is keeping a fast pace, increase the DC by 5. Weather conditions (see [[#Weather]] in this chapter) might also affect the difficulty of overland travel.
>[!rules] PLAYER ADVICE
>Remember that PCs with the [[012. Worldbuilding/Traits/Talents/Far Traveler]] talent have advantage on WIS ([[Survival]]) checks to determine if the party becomes lost.

#### Obstacles
An obstacle is terrain or weather that obstructs the characters’ path. Examples include a cliff, a blizzard, or a forest fire. To get past the obstacle, characters might need to backtrack and find an alternate route, or they might need to take shelter until the obstacle goes away. Let the players spend time thinking about a solution, then be generous in adjudicating whether their plan works.

In addition to the chance of a delay (adding a few hours, a day, or a couple of days to the current stage of the journey), here are some other consequences you can impose if characters fail to overcome or bypass an obstacle:

**Combat Encounter.** The characters encounter one or more Hostile creatures. For example, marching through a burning forest instead of circling around it might prompt an encounter with raging fire elementals.

**Damage.** The characters take damage. For example, a character who tumbles over a waterfall might take Bludgeoning damage. See “[Improvising Damage](https://www.dndbeyond.com/sources/dnd/dmg-2024/running-the-game#ImprovisingDamage)” in this chapter for guidelines on determining how much damage is appropriate.

**Exhaustion.** The obstacle fatigues the characters, causing them to gain [[Exhaustion]] levels. For example, marching through a blizzard instead of taking shelter might cause each character to gain 1d4 [[Exhaustion]] levels.

**Another Condition.** The obstacle imposes another condition on the characters. For example, wading through a fetid swamp rather than skirting around it might impose the [[Poisoned]] condition, which lasts until removed by magic.

#### Searches
This challenge often comes up in the last stage of a journey: the characters have to find their destination, which might be an island, an old mine, an ancient ruin, a magical pool, a hag’s cottage, or some other feature.

The Search DC column of the [[05. Advanced Exploration#Travel Terrain|Travel Terrain]] table suggests DCs for Wisdom ([[Perception]]) checks made to find something in different types of terrain. You can adjust these DCs based on the specific terrain features and the nature of what the characters are trying to find, using the guidelines for setting DCs earlier in this chapter.
## Traveling Activities
The rules for travel in the *Player’s Guide* cover the basics, such as pace and the dangers of forced marches, but the following additional rules allow you to expand on travel in the game.
>[!rules] TRAVELING AS EXPLORATION
>Rules for travel are most useful when abstracting long journeys where the PCs travel across vast stretches of environment. Since it likely isn’t interesting to describe each day of trekking through the same gigantic forest, the rules instead give a sense of how fast the party travels while interacting with random encounters.
### Tracking
While traveling at a normal or slow pace, a PC can follow the tracks of another creature. Tracking involves making a WIS ([[Survival]]) check when the GM agrees a check is necessary. Full tracking rules are detailed in the Player’s Guide.
>[!rules] PLAYER ADVICE
>Remember that PCs with the 1st-level ranger feature Explorer always have advantage on checks made to track creatures.
### Foraging
While traveling at a normal or slow pace, a PC can make a WIS ([[Survival]]) check to forage for food and water as they move. PCs can’t forage while moving at a fast pace. The DC for the check is dependent on the availability of food and water as noted on the **Foraging** table.

```tx
|FOOD AND WATER SCARCITY|SURVIVAL CHECK DC|
|---|---|
|Abundant food and water (such as traveling overland during the summer or autumn during peacetime).|10|
|Limited food and water (such as traveling overland during the winter or spring or traveling overland in summer or autumn during wartime).|15|
|Scarce food and water (such as traveling underground or traveling overland in winter or spring during wartime).|20|
[FORAGING #css/tx/table]
```

The check assumes that one PC is foraging. On a failed check, the forager finds no food or water. On a success, they find enough food and water for rations equal to 1d6 + WIS modifier.

A ration provides food and water for one Small or Medium creature. Large creatures (such as horses or unusually big PCs) require double rations. Horses and pack animals might be able to graze, depending on the terrain, but PCs must always consider the needs of their animals when they forage.

If multiple PCs forage, the PC with the highest Survival bonus can make the check with advantage. On a successful check, the foraging PCs find rations equal to 1d6 + the total of all the foragers’ WIS modifiers.

A PC can go without food for a number of days equal to 3 + their CON modifier (minimum 1). At the end of each day beyond that limit, they automatically suffer one level of exhaustion. A normal day of eating resets the count of days without food to zero.

PCs can make food last longer by subsisting on half rations. Eating a half ration in a day counts as half a day without food. The first half-day, the character suffers no penalty, but half-days accrue into full days, so the PCs still suffer exhaustion as the days accumulate until they consume a full ration.

You can easily adapt these foraging rules for other survival resources found in nature, such as firewood, medicinal herbs, or crafting materials.

**Foraging in Extreme Environments**. Foraging in extreme environments, such as desert or arctic tundra, is more difficult. The DC to forage in such an environment remains the same, but the amount of available food and water is reduced, and animals might need more consideration. On a successful WIS (Survival) check in an extreme environment, a foraging PC finds rations equal to their WIS modifier.

Creatures traveling in such environments might also have to contend with environmental hazards like extreme cold or heat (see Chapter 7).
>[!rules] SURVIVAL FANTASY
>Many fantasy roleplaying campaigns downplay the need for provisions while on the road or disregard it completely. Low-circle spells such as [[create or destroy water]], [[Goodberry]], and [[purify food and drink]] make it unlikely that PCs will starve or get dehydrated on the road. The 3rd-circle spell create food and water trivializes survival concerns by producing enough provisions to cover the typical adventuring party as well as any mounts they have.
>
>If you want to introduce wilderness survival as a challenge or theme in your campaign, consider removing these spells from the game or increase their circle by one to force players to make harder choices about what spells they cast. Regardless, seek consensus from your players before altering the game rules.
