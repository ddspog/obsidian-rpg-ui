Each campaign you run takes place in a world of your own design. Even if you use a published setting, the moment you interpret what’s written or make a decision, the world changes and becomes your own. The act of fleshing out that world—its metaphysics and the peoples and cultures within it—is worldbuilding. 

As a GM, worldbuilding is primarily your responsibility, and this chapter serves as a primer on it to enhance your campaign.

# Civilization
![[civilization.webp|right|384]]Civilization encompasses the culture and way of life of a particular people in a particular area: such as tribes of neolithic human hunter-gatherers, nomadic bands of elves roaming subterranean hell-worlds, or bustling metropolises filled with giants. Even those who purposefully excise themselves from society or define themselves in opposition to it are shaped by the civilized lands surrounding them.
## Building Settlements
People live in settlements—and though the term is normally used in reference to sedentary peoples, nomadic populations deserve similar consideration. Settlements can range from tiny fishing villages to sprawling industrial cities. When creating a settlement, ask how your players will interact with it. Who will they meet there? What might they do there? What does this place need or want from them? What can they get from it? How is that shaped by its history or purpose?
### Purpose
Civilizations are the fabric of a campaign. They define a norm that events diverge from and threaten, a background for NPCs and plots, a location for adventures, and a baseline for stakes. They are omnipresent, and investment in the setting is tied to the interest they garner.

As such, the purpose of a civilization in your game world is to shape and enable the adventures you have within it.

If the logical or realistic answer to a question goes against what you want for your campaign, then toss it out and ask questions to find an answer that works for you. 
### Types
There are two primary types of fantasy settlements: sedentary and nomadic. 
#### Sedentary 
Sedentary peoples have most of their population living in one location. They can support large populations—and high population densities—through agriculture and permanent infrastructure. These settlements require arable land, water, and other resources to maintain themselves and are limited in their size and placement by geography. Well-established settlements are more densely populated, and smaller settlements arise near them. Those settlements that are more recent, are more dangerous, or have access to fewer resources will be smaller. 

In your game world, sedentary settlements might have more or fewer restrictions on where they can thrive. Settlements might require copious access to magic, might cluster behind walls to hide from monsters, or might sustain themselves in inhospitable environments through magical infrastructure. 
#### Nomadic 
Nomadic peoples have few or no permanent settlements. Instead, they travel periodically (or constantly), normally within a known, defined territory they might or might not view as exclusively theirs. Nomadic civilizations tie themselves to mobile assets, like livestock, so they have different requirements than sedentary peoples. 

Nomads typically arise in marginally habitable areas where living conditions can’t support dense populations, such as steppes, deserts, and other semi-arid regions where traditional agriculture isn’t possible. They rely on larger expanses or multiple regions for pastureland, hunting grounds, and water. Most nomads have well-established and stable migration routes throughout the year that run through the territory they claim. 

In your game world, such peoples might be nomadic for any number of reasons and migrate in different ways. For example, flying humanoids (or humanoids with flying mounts) might migrate along mountaintops or forests canopies. Migration routes might stretch underground or into other planes entirely. Their migrations might follow magical phenomena or avoid powerful, roaming monsters. 
#### Size 
The size of a settlement—in terms of both the physical space it occupies and its population—determines much about its needs and capabilities. 
###### Small 
Small settlements are the most common. Through much of the real world’s history, before the modern era, the majority of any given population lived in settlements like this. Nomadic peoples also tend toward the smaller as the resources needed to support a larger population are likely scarcer. 

Small settlements, such as hamlets and villages, rely on agriculture (to a presumably sustainable degree) and resource extraction to survive. For most, this is food production, and the settlement produces enough to sustain itself then sells what remains. Other settlements are instead devoted to mining, salt harvesting, woodcutting, and the like, which are largely exported, so they might require the import of some necessities like food. 

In your game world, small settlements might be dedicated to the production of magical resources rather than having more mundane livelihoods. In regions with particularly dangerous wildlife, there might be very few small settlements as the need for defense against threats encourages people to cluster together into larger settlements. In pastoral or wooded regions, settlements might be repositories of the strange and supernatural compared to the more urbane existences of large cities. 

**_Hamlet._** A hamlet is the smallest type of settlement. It covers those so small that they have no infrastructure aside from the houses people live in. They normally consist of less than 100 inhabitants. Many hamlets are extended family groups and subsist entirely on farming or herding. Even when not related, everyone in a hamlet knows each other. These settlements tend to be diffuse, made of households spread across a fairly wide area. They tend not to have any sort of centralized meeting place, and gatherings occur instead at someone’s home. 

**_Villages._** Villages are much like hamlets, though large enough to develop a dense urban center. They can grow to around 1,000 inhabitants. The village center includes any communally important buildings (such as a meeting hall, temple, blacksmith, or inn), a market, and some homes. In farming and herding villages, homes are spread out more across various fields and farms around the village center. 
###### Large 
Large settlements might arise over time as one or more smaller settlements expand and merge. For more nomadic peoples, they might only be populated for part of the year, having only a small permanent population that swells with many thousands of visitors during festivals, political events, and other gatherings of smaller nomadic groups. Their architecture is often built to accommodate this, with clearings for vast herds of livestock and entire districts of temporary structures that arise overnight, but sustaining a city-sized population as a coherent nomadic group is incredibly difficult and tends not to happen outside of war.
  
In your game world, large settlements can showcase all the most unique and fantastical elements of the setting. Their wealth might allow them to host universities of wizards or orders of mystics. Their reach might see elves, humans, and orcs living side by side in daily life, magical creatures pulling carts down city streets, and enchanted baubles being sold openly in the market or integrated into city infrastructure. 

The nature of a large settlement can also change entirely in such a setting. Nomads might build cities onto the backs of wandering behemoths that migrate along their traditional routes. Enchanted cities might hang in mid-air or suspend themselves between the planes. Magical infrastructure might grow food or create water from nothing, allowing even greater population densities and city structures. 

**_Town._** A town is a settlement large enough that most of its population doesn’t derive their living from resource extraction. They might instead work in industry, trade, administration, and other more specialized fields. Towns have at least 1,000 inhabitants and up to 10,000. Towns are built around a town center, which hosts government buildings, communal meeting places, and often some sort of public square. These settlements are densely packed with shops and houses crammed together around streets meant for pedestrian use. They tend to be fairly compact and surrounded by farms for miles in all directions. The size of a town mandates a more dedicated government than smaller settlements require, and most have some sort of council or mayoral position to legislate. The higher population density of a town limits where they can be built, but traditionally, they arise at important crossroads or river or border crossings. They rely on trade to import raw materials and food and export various finished goods. Towns are often the largest settlement for many miles, and they connect smaller settlements in the area to the rest of the world. In times of crisis, people from many miles around might flee to the relative safety of the town’s walls. 

**_City._** A city is a town large and important enough to receive special status. This is a political consideration as much as a demographic one, and towns might be designated cities with only a few thousand inhabitants. There is no upper limit on how many people a city can hold, though pre-modern cities were much smaller than modern cities, and they rarely grew larger than 30,000 inhabitants. Notable exceptions in the pre-modern era could have as many as 200,000, but these massive cities were exceptionally rare. Cities escalate every element of town life: more of the population have urban careers, the government is more formalized and present in everyday life, roads and buildings are bigger, and people visit from even farther away. The most important buildings in a city are often architectural marvels, meant to show the wealth and power of the city, while their markets boast a staggering array of goods. Merchants and travelers from other countries are common, and cities are often the only place one can buy the most complex of goods. This also limits where cities can be built, however, since they require more trade, more food, more water, and more people to maintain it all. More area is cleared for farmland and pasture outside of the walls, and often, the city must set up some system to deal with sewage and waste. As such, cities typically arise where several favorable factors converge, such as an intersection of arable land, large trade routes, and major waterways.
## Quick Settlements
![[quick-settlements.webp]]
When you need a settlement quickly, use the following tables to generate one. The Settlements by Size table provides population ranges for villages, towns, and cities as well as the value of the most expensive item the settlement is likely to have for sale. Adjust these numbers as you wish to account for special circumstances. For example, aPotion of Healing (which costs 50 GP) is too expensive an item to purchase in most villages, but a village that happens to have an alchemist, an herbalist, or a potion brewer might have one or more such potions for sale.
