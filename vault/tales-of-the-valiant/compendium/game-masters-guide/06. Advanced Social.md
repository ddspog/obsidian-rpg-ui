This chapter expands on the basic roleplaying guidance contained in the _Player’s Guide_. Within, you’ll find tools to create and portray intriguing NPCs of all types. You’ll also find strategies for effectively incorporating NPC factions and villains into the world of your campaign. The chapter ends with two optional subsystems—attitude and status—that allow you to concretely represent a party’s social standing. 
# NPCs in a Campaign 
The best adventures have memorable characters—NPCs that help or hinder, monsters that are more than just bags of hit points to be killed, and delightful tavern patrons and shopkeepers. Primary, secondary, and background NPCs can make an adventure sing and can make a game world feel alive. 
## NPC Roles
When creating NPCs, it’s helpful to start by deciding what role you expect them to play in relation to the PCs: allies, antagonists, and background characters are three major categories in which most NPCs can be sorted. (A fourth category is quest giver, but this can overlap with any of the others.) When you have limited time to prepare, keeping these roles in mind helps you spend your time well. A person meant to be a background NPC doesn’t warrant the same detail given to an NPC intended to serve as a longtime ally or recurring villain. 

The tables in this section are largely for inspiration. You will often already know the details of important NPCs, but occasionally, you might need a helping hand. Use the tables according to the needs of your evolving campaign.
### Allies
Your PCs might recruit others to help them in their cause, or they might meet NPCs along the way that have aligned goals. The most important thing to remember when creating allies is that they are there to support the PCs, not overshadow them. A good ally can suggest a course of action, bring information to the table, or help take down a monster, but they shouldn’t be the ones making the decisions or landing the killing blows.

### Background NPCs
Background NPCs are likely not vital to the plot but present opportunities to provide players with information, quests, or roleplaying encounters. While these NPCs don’t need as much attention as allies or antagonists, having a handful of them prepared in case the PCs take a sudden interest in one keeps the game fun and lively. At a minimum, have a cheat sheet of names and of physical and personality traits handy.

### Quest Givers
Arguably one of the most important NPCs in an adventure is the quest giver. In general, keep your quest givers honest, or at least predictable. You want players to trust that they’ll get whatever reward you promised via the NPC. If you teach players not to trust quest givers from the start, then they’ll be suspicious of every NPC you put in front of them. Besides, it’s such a common “twist” for the quest giver to be something other than what they seem, that many players expect it. This doesn’t mean that every quest giver needs to be a stock character, but in general, save your surprises for the dungeon. 

Roll a d20 and consult the **Quest Giver** table for some quick options to fill this role.

### Wants
What does this NPC want, and what are they willing to do to get it? A bored guard who only exists to fight the PCs is less interesting than a bored guard who wants to be done with work in order to go pick up their daughter or to take their spouse out for dinner. A villain who is evil just for the sake of being evil is fine, but a villain with a more personal reason for their deeds creates an adventure that hits a little deeper.

## Roleplaying NPCs
Lifeless NPCs fade into the background, serving as mere vehicles to dispense information or sell pints of ale. Sometimes, that’s enough. For times when you want more, this section provides basic guidance to help you bring NPCs to life in meaningful ways. 
### Know their Motivations
The fastest way to make an NPC meaningful is to understand what motivates them. Knowing what they need and want, knowing their fears and dreams, helps you to determine what choices they make, both when interacting with the PCs and behind the scenes. 

Motivations can be simple, like an innkeeper raising money for a summer home, or very complicated, like a villain dealing with a devil to avenge their slaughtered family. Whatever the scope, fully understanding an NPC’s desires breeds richer interactions. This is just as important as the PCs’ motivations, and when the two align or conflict, everything gets exciting.
### Allow them to Grow
NPC motivations can—and often should—change. PCs usually discover a lot about themselves and how they fit into the world throughout a campaign, and if the NPCs evolve in the same way, they feel like real pieces of the developing story. Tools like attitude and status (found later in this chapter) are helpful ways to mechanically track some of these changes. 
### Make them relatable
The fantasy genre is built on a pyramid of character tropes: flirtatious bards, forgetful wizards, and rogues from broken families abound. They exist because they are fun and relatable, and you should definitely use them. However, if every NPC in your campaign follows a common trope, the fun can wear off, and if every NPC is subverted as a complicated re-envisioning of the genre, they then lose their relatability. 

Just as you should insert safe havens among the dangers of the campaign, inserting uncomplicated—or “standard”— NPCs gives players somewhere to rest their expectations. A simple guideline for this is to put in two tropes for every trope-breaker. After you present a stock noble king and his naive princess, make his queen conniving, though with a soft spot for animals. Players can lean into the more trope-worn NPCs while they grapple with the twists of complex ones. As you gain experience, you can switch things up, but generally speaking, let the standard, uncomplicated NPCs outnumber the complex ones by at least two to one. 

In addition, keep in mind that unusual things only stand out because of their environment. A village of predictable peasants makes the braggart pig farmer more interesting and unusual by comparison. Ultimately, a well-mixed band of NPCs makes for a more enjoyable play experience.

>[!rules] TIPS FOR RUNNING NPCS
>Here are some tips for managing and running NPCs: 
>- Avoid scenes involving heavy interactions between NPCs. If it’s unavoidable, assign a primary NPC to lead the scene. That NPC does most of the talking while the secondary NPCs only provide commentary. 
>- Don’t get attached to an NPC until you know how your players feel about them. Discard NPCs your players don’t strongly react to. You can try again with a different NPC later. 
>- If an NPC is traveling with the party, let a player run them in combat or other dice-rolling situations. Only ask for help from a player who has a firm grasp of how to run their own character though. 
>- If your players react strongly to an NPC (whether they love or hate them), bring them back later! A collection of familiar and interesting NPCs at your disposal keeps things fun, maintains continuity, and saves you from having to create brand-new NPCs repeatedly. 
>- When you create an NPC and your players react strongly to them, immediately write down what they look and sound like. Notes about whether they spoke with an accent, perhaps at a particular pace or volume, or using specific catchphrases help you recall the character when you need them again in a couple of weeks.
