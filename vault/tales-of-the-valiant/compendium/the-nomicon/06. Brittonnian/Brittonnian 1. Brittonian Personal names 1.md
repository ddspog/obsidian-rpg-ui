>[!rules] FORCED DIALECTS
One way to emphasize a particular culture or to differentiate between them is to hammer home a basic language difference. This works for gaming; a novelist will want to be more subtle. Here is a way to really emphasize a linguistic difference.
>- **Method 1**: Pick a particular name-starting syllable, such as “Ara.” If the tables for your culture just have two or three letters (such as A, Ae, Be, Ca, Cai), you can use that or add a workable second syllable using ra, re, be, ca, etc. This might give you something like “Beca.” Now, EVERY PERSON of that gender has a name starting with that initial two syllables. Roll for the second part, but always use the “Beca” prefix until you get sick of it. Becalaith, Becaveen, Becagid, Becannait, etcetera. The players, unlike normal, will actually get the sense that this is a country with its own independent culture. It’s a bit like distilling the ordinary mix of linguistic differences into a 190-proof slug of purified difference.
>- **Method 2**: Like method 1, this approach takes a group of consonants from the middle of a normal name, but then uses that mix of consonants for EVERYONE of that particular gender. Look through the naming table (in the endings of names) for something like “ng,” “rh,” or “dw.” Work that consonant cluster into every single name. You can even use exactly the same ending for all the names. For example, looking at the Brittonian Masculine Personal Names (Gaelic) Tables, there’s an ending of “nndubán.” There are also several endings that start with “nn,” and you could restrict all names to the ones with the “nn.” But let’s say you’re going to go whole hog on this idea. All male names in this country end with “nndubán.” The players will meet Conndubán, Crinndubán, Danndubán, Dianndubán, Enndubán, Faenndubán, and so on. Amazingly, many players won’t even notice exactly what’s happening. They will perceive the country as being unique, but they won’t quite know why.
