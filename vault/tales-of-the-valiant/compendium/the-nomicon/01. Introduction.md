# The Nomicon
![[disclaimer.webp|right|384]]Be aware that even the real-world "naming groups" in this book aren't accurate in a linguistic, historical, or cultural sense, even taking into account that they are random-generation tables of "nonsense" names.

In order to make some of the naming groups "work" for an English-speaking world builder without any background in linguistics, I have generally spelled things in a way that can be pronounced easily rather than accurately, and I have lumped several languages and time periods together in ways that will make trained linguists and historians despair. As examples, the "Sequatorial" naming group draws on several unrelated language families in Africa, and the "Arabish" naming group includes a lot of phoneme patterns from Persian/Farsi, which is in an entirely different linguistic family (an Indo-European language) from Arabic (which is a Semitic language). As to time periods, these are often overgeneralized as well. In England, the Middle Ages began with more than one "Isolated Celtic" languages, and went through Anglo-Saxon, Old English, and got all the way into Middle English as the predominant languages, all in the space of eight hundred years, all of which is arguably in "the Middle Ages." The time period is so long, and contained so much social tumult, that there's almost no generalization you can accurately make about the whole time period of the Middle Ages, even sticking to Europe alone.

In other words, I have almost entirely sacrificed good scholarship in favor of poetics and useability. There's no cultural appropriation in here, formally speaking, because there's no meaningful content in the name tables other than whatever's accidentally generated. Sounding "like" a culture's language doesn't appropriate anything, although it could be used to caricature the culture, a risk hopefully to be avoided by the users of this book. But you shouldn't use this book as a guide to understanding linguistics or history, because that's not what it's about. It's about names that sound right, are evocative, and are (relatively) easy to pronounce. Names that work well in a fantasy world.
# What Makes This Book Different from Other Books of Fantasy Names?
## Place Names
First of all, the Nomicon is designed for the GM as much as for the player, so it focuses heavily on place names as well as character names. As a companion book to the Tome of Worldbuilding, it's designed to help the GM generate names for cities, countries, civilizations, and terrain features such as forests and marshes.
## Generators More Than Lists
Secondly, the Nomicon contains mostly name generators rather than lists of names. There's an upside and a downside to this approach. The upside is that the book "contains" more names than a book consisting of lists, by an order of magnitude. It also means that if your objective is to generate a "new" name that's not historically based, you'll only generate a historical name by coincidence. The downside is that since the names are generated, they have a higher chance of stumbling on something that sounds "wrong" in the language being emulated. For example, the author Clark Ashton Smith wrote a series of short stories about an imaginary Medieval French province called Averoigne. Smith's French came from books rather than from conversation, so he didn't realize that "Averoigne" is almost unpronounceable in spoken French, even though it's derived from absolutely authentic French phonemes. The same possibility exists with these tables.
# Omissions
There are a couple of major language groups omitted from this book, namely Mesoamerican and Chinese. Chinese is omitted because it simply doesn't work to try and generate phonemes for a tonal language. The East-Asiantic naming group does include a lot of the Chinese-language phonemes that worked their way into the languages of Southeast Asia (and as far as East Africa), but there's not a specifically Chinese-emulating set of tables included.

The Mesoamerican languages are omitted because I'm simply not well enough versed in how those languages sound. I've got enough familiarity with African languages (if one can even say that about a group that includes over a thousand languages) to manage how the phonemes interact, but I really don't have enough feel for the Mesoamerican languages to even try.
# How to Use the Book
## Naming Groups
For the most part this book is self-explanatory, but it may need an introduction to the idea of the naming groups (Anglish, Arabish, Brittonian, and so forth). These aren't historical names, although some of the names in the lists are real ones. They are built from common phonemes (sound groups) from the base languages in the group, so they sound "right" for an invented name. This approach is designed to help the world-builder avoid a common problem with naming, which is to draw on so many different cultures for names that none of the countries have a distinctive sound to them.

In some cases, you simply won't care about getting that specific, or it might present too much of a decision-making process when you're focused on something else (like building great political intrigues or placing interesting dungeons). In that case, for a European-style sound, you can use the Eurovesian tables, which draw from a lot of source languages.

## Formulas versus Tables
Many of the tables are preceded by a list of formulas. Formulas are mentioned if the results from a table need other words or table results combined with them. For example, a street named after a profession, such as "Alchemists" could be formulated in several different ways. It might be the "Street of Alchemists," or the "Avenue of Alchemists" (calling on a table of street types), and it might also be "Alchemist Street" (using the table result of "Alchemist") in a different order of words. The formulas give you ideas for different presentations of the words from the tables; they are very important, because they create variation.
